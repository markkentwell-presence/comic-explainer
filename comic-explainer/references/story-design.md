# From transcript to comic

## Start with the behaviour

Ask what the audience should do differently afterwards. “Understand the system” is less useful than “ask the three qualification questions before booking the appointment.” Keep the speaker's intent and words; do not invent evidence or testimonials.

## A useful training arc

| Beat | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Familiar friction | Let people recognise the problem | A third meeting ends without a decision. |
| Reframe | Open a gap in the expected explanation | More activity can still leave the cup empty. |
| Physical metaphor | Give the team a picture they can recall | Damp soil versus usable water. |
| Practical options | Turn the picture into choices | Choose the tool that addresses the barrier. |
| Rehearsal | Give them language they can try | Practise one specific question. |
| Next move | Shrink the change and anchor the habit | One task, one person, one review date. |

Use this as a shape, not a rigid scene count. A short how-to might be just four scenes: start, common mistake, corrected action, finished result.

## What belongs where

- **Image:** one relationship or action; a few memorable labels.
- **Headline:** the judgement, in plain words.
- **Takeaway:** the sentence someone can repeat later.
- **Pop-out:** a caveat, example, exact script or extra decision detail.
- **Presenter notes:** nuance, story context, delivery emphasis and questions.

An abstract concept gets a concrete image; a complex qualification gets words. Do not squeeze every caveat into a picture.

## Pace and practice

Budget time before expanding the notes. For a 15-minute talk, nine scenes need an average of about 100 seconds, including brief pauses. Spend more time on the conceptual turn and the practical rehearsal. Make the sum explicit. A long group exercise needs extra time or a shorter talk.

End with a small practice move rather than another overview. Name the action and its trigger. If the setting permits it, make the successful behaviour visible in the next existing team review.

## Keep the metaphor honest

An illustration should simplify without becoming a false rule. A depth is not a valuation; an arrow does not prove causation; a fictional example is not a documented result. Explain where the metaphor stops, usually in notes. Leave room for the correct decision to be “this does not fit the goal.”
